# Reading: how large are real climate to conflict links

Bounded reading, 2026-11-09. One question: where do published climate to
conflict effects sit against our detection limit (blind search hears a
planted link only at strength about 6, about 60% of events in its cells; a
targeted test hears strength 2, about 4%, and not 1.4, about 0.6%)?

## 1. Syntheses

- **Hsiang, Burke and Miguel 2013, Science** (author copy, CC BY): "for each
  1 standard deviation (1σ) change in climate towards warmer temperatures or
  more extreme rainfall, median estimates indicate that the frequency of
  interpersonal violence rises 4% and the frequency of intergroup conflict
  rises 14%." Median across studies of many scales (village to country, day
  to year, all regions). Read at source.
- **Burke, Hsiang and Miguel 2015, Annual Review of Economics** (NBER
  abstract only): "each 1σ increase toward warmer temperatures increasing
  the frequency of contemporaneous interpersonal conflict by 2.4% and of
  intergroup conflict by 11.3%", and "the 2-period cumulative effect of
  rainfall on intergroup conflict is also substantial (3.5%/σ)." 55 studies.
- **Contested.** Buhaug et al. 2014, Climatic Change (abstract via
  EconBiz): the meta-analysis "suffers from shortcomings with respect to
  sample selection and analytical coherence", and a modified assessment
  "suggests that scientific research on climate and conflict to date has
  produced mixed and inconclusive results." The authors' reply (abstract
  seen only in a search summary, not checked) calls the reanalysis wrong.
- **Mach et al. 2019, Nature, expert elicitation.** Abstract (Europe PMC):
  experts "agree that climate has affected organized armed conflict within
  countries", but "low socioeconomic development and low capabilities of the
  state, are judged to be substantially more influential". The numbers come
  from a Lancaster University press release only (secondary; the paper's
  text was not reachable): "3 to 20 per cent of the risk of violent armed
  conflict within countries has been influenced by climate" over the last
  century; at 2°C "rising to a 13 per cent chance", at 4°C "a 26 per cent
  chance" (of a substantial climate influence on conflict risk).

## 2. Grid-cell and sub-national Africa

- **Harari and La Ferrara**, "Conflict, Climate and Cells" (2012 working
  paper, read at source; the 2018 REStat version was not checked), 1° cells,
  Africa 1997-2011, yearly incidence: "A spell of SPEI that is one standard
  deviation below the mean throughout the growing season is associated with
  a 4 percentage point increase in conflict likelihood in the subsequent
  year and in the year following that; this is roughly one fourth of the
  mean of dependent variable." Same paper, conflict history: conflict last
  year in the cell gives "a 33 percentage point increase", about eight times
  the drought effect.
- **O'Loughlin et al. 2012, PNAS**, East Africa, 1° cells, 1990-2009, event
  counts (full text, PMC): wet anomaly "0.815 relative risk ratio"; "a 29.6%
  increase in predicted violent events" at 2σ hotter than normal, while
  the same paper has 1σ hotter lowering risk by 12%, so the effect is not a
  straight line; and "the actual contribution of SPI6 and TI6 to predicting
  violence in the study area is modest when combined with other factors."
- **O'Loughlin et al. 2014, PNAS**, sub-Saharan Africa 1980-2012 (abstract):
  "Precipitation deviations, both high and low, are generally not
  significant." No per-SD number taken.
- **von Uexkull et al. 2016, PNAS** (abstract): drought "under most
  conditions has little effect" on group rebellion, but raises sustained
  violence for agriculture-dependent and politically excluded groups in
  very poor countries. Ethnic-group unit, no per-SD number in the abstract.

## 3. Out-of-sample skill over conflict history

- **Michelini, Šedová, Schewe and Frieler 2023, Humanities and Social
  Sciences Communications** (full text read): mainland Africa 1994-2012,
  one-year-ahead incidence at country and two sub-national levels, random
  forests. "fully specified forecast models including conflict history,
  governance, and socio-economic variables are not improved by adding
  extreme weather impacts information"; weather adds "almost no information
  to governance variables or conflict history". Weather alone predicts
  "modestly well". Results shown as ROC and PR curves; no single number
  quoted here.
- ViEWS, its prediction competition, Hegre et al., Vesco et al.: **not
  found** in this reading with a climate-versus-history comparison. Not
  checked.

## 4. Reading against our scale

Read as a rate ratio per SD, published average effects sit at about
**1.03 to 1.15**: 1.035 (rainfall), 1.11 to 1.14 (temperature, intergroup).
O'Loughlin's figure is about 1.3 at 2σ heat only (no per-σ figure is taken
from it, since 1σ lowers risk there). The largest
local figure, Harari and La Ferrara's, is about **1.25** in incidence, and
only for a whole growing season at least 1 SD dry. **Every one is below
1.4, our weakest strength, which the targeted test could not hear.**
(O'Loughlin's 1.3 and Harari and La Ferrara's 1.25 are the nearest.) None
is near 6, where blind search starts to hear. On the share scale, Mach's
3 to 20% of risk sits between our strengths 2 and 3.5, but that is expert
judgement over a century and all places, not a measured per-cell share,
and it is press-release only. The translation is rough in five ways:
published effects are mostly yearly incidence (any event, yes or no), not
monthly event counts; the meta-analyses pool very different scales;
"per SD" there is linear, while our strength applies past a threshold;
averages hide subgroups where effects are larger (von Uexkull); and our
event shares depend on how often the generator's input crosses its
threshold. The forecasting result agrees from the other side: once
conflict history is in, weather adds almost nothing out of sample. Plainly:
if real links are this size, we should not expect blind search on our grid
to find them, and even a targeted test would be at or below its floor.
